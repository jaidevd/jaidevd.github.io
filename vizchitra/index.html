Caption This

Teaching Machines to Narrate Charts

A chart compresses data. A caption compresses the chart. This talk is about whether machines can learn that second compression — and why generating a caption turns out to be the easy part.

Jaidev Deshpande  ·  beingbayesian.in

VizChitra 2026, Bangalore

Hi, I'm Jaidev. I write data essays — charts, numbers, the occasional rant — at a place called beingbayesian. Today I want to talk about the smallest part of any chart: the part we write last and think about least. The caption. Here's the one idea I'll keep coming back to. A chart compresses data. A caption compresses the chart. And it turns out that getting a machine to write that caption is the easy part. The hard part is everything around it — and that's what I want to show you.

Captions are subjective

There’s no wrong caption — but a good chart and a suitable caption make a good story.

Good: “Men cook a little bit, but they certainly don’t clean up after.”

Questions on Stack Overflow

Obvious: “ChatGPT killed Stack Overflow.”

Good: “Good, unique questions were anyway on the decline. ChatGPT, at best, was beating an already‑dead horse.”

Obvious: “Foreign liquor is the most expensive intoxicant.”

Good: “Whether you’re insured or not doesn’t make more than Rs 15 worth of difference.”

Same data and the same charts. It’s the caption — the few words that you write accompanying the chart — that is deeply contextual.

Now — there's no single right answer here. That's the whole point. There's no wrong caption, exactly, but a good chart with the right caption is what turns numbers into a story. Look at the difference. For the kitchen one, the obvious caption is "women spend much more time in the kitchen." True, but flat. The better one: men do cook a little — but they certainly don't clean up after. For Stack Overflow, the obvious one is "ChatGPT killed it." The better one: the good questions were already dying; ChatGPT was beating an already-dead horse. And for the intoxicants chart: yes, foreign liquor is the priciest — but the honest reading is that whether you're insured or not barely changes anything, maybe fifteen rupees. Same data, same charts. The only thing that changed is the sentence — and that sentence is where all the judgment lives. Which raises the obvious question…

The big question

Can we get a machine to write good captions?

From the information (the data) and the intent (the chart) — can an AI do it?

And how would it deal with subjectivity?

So here's the question for the next twenty minutes. Can we get a machine to write a *good* caption — not a correct one, a *good* one. It has two things to go on: the information, which is the data, and the intent, which is the chart someone chose to draw. And here's the part that actually kept me up: captions are subjective. There's no single right answer. So how would a machine ever deal with *that*? Let's build up to it.

Every insight is a chain of compressions

Together, these three are one triplet — data, chart, caption. The caption is the smallest, most contextual compression of all.

The first conversion — data → chart — is free; machines already do it. The trick is getting from chart → caption.

Let me reframe what's really going on when you make a chart. You start with data — here, two hundred-odd months of Stack Overflow numbers. You compress that into a picture: one chart. Then you compress the chart into a sentence: the insight. There's a line I love from Gregory Chaitin — comprehension *is* compression. To understand something is just to find a shorter description of it. A good chart is shorter than its data; a good caption is shorter than its chart. And notice: the first step, data to chart, machines already do for free — every BI tool on earth does it. The interesting, unsolved step is the last one. Chart to caption.

Which caption belongs to which chart? Pairing them up is its own problem — for a person, and for a machine.

Before a machine can write a caption, here's an even more basic question: can it even tell which caption *goes with* which chart? Picture it like this — a pile of charts, a pile of captions, all jumbled together. Match them up. For us it's almost insultingly easy; you read one, you read the other, you pair them. For a machine, it turns out, pairing them up is a genuine problem. So I tested it.

They don’t line up

Match each chart to its caption by similarity, across a corpus of ~23,000 charts:

The title here is the heading drawn on the chart itself — not the caption we’re trying to find. A caption names the axis, not the topic, and to a generic vision model a chart is just shapes, not meaning — so chart and caption don’t land near each other until that title bridges them. The chart→caption compression doesn’t run backwards off the shelf.

Comprehending the image of a chart isn’t enough — models need to be grounded in data and semantics.

And they don't line up. I took about twenty-three thousand charts and asked a model to find the right caption for each one, by similarity. From the shape of the chart alone — just the pixels — it gets it right about a third of one percent of the time. That's chance. Add the underlying data, and it climbs to seven percent. The real jump comes when you add the title printed *on* the chart — then it's thirty-eight percent. And that's the tell: to a generic vision model, a chart is just shapes. A caption names the axis, not the topic. So the chart and its caption don't even land near each other until that title bridges them. Reading the *picture* isn't enough — the model has to be grounded in the data and the words.

the caption still needs the data

The chart is a lossy compression. To write a faithful caption, the model still has to reach back to the data — the picture alone isn’t enough.

Which brings us to the uncomfortable part. Same pipeline — data, chart, caption. The natural assumption is: to caption a chart, look at the chart. But the chart is a *lossy* compression — drawing the data already threw information away. So if you want a caption that's actually faithful, the model can't just read the picture. It has to reach back, past the chart, to the data itself. The picture alone isn't enough.

So here's the simplest thing that could possibly work. Give the model both — the chart *and* the data behind it — and ask it to write the caption. That's the whole pipeline. No fine-tuning, nothing clever, not yet. Chart plus data in, caption out. Everything I show you from here is a variation on this one move.

To do any of this seriously, you need data — a lot of charts, with their numbers *and* good captions. That didn't exist, so I built it. It's called PlotCaptions: about a hundred and fifty thousand charts, the table behind every one, and roughly eighteen million captions. It's built on top of PlotQA, a visual question-answering dataset from 2020. PlotQA has these templated reasoning and comparison questions for each chart — I turned every question-and-answer pair back into a caption, and then, because the grammar was frankly a mess, ran the whole lot through a grammar checker. Each entry is one triplet: a chart, its data, and a caption that's actually true of it.

But what makes a caption good? Another LLM judges it

Its brief

  • Parse the chart’s underlying data
  • Infer the intent from the chart’s elements — title, axes, series
  • Weigh its impact, not just whether it’s accurate

Few-shotted with intense & boring captions

★“Georgia’s farm output jumped ~61× in a single year, 1993→1994.”🤯
★“Uruguay’s value more than doubled in one year — the largest jump of any country.”🤯
×“Life expectancy rose every year, from 78.35 to 79.1.”😒
×“The legal‑rights index stayed constant at 7 across all years.”😒

A handful of labelled examples calibrate it — then it scores every caption the same way. This judge is the instrument behind every impact score that follows.

Generator: Claude Sonnet 4.6  ·  Judge: Claude Opus 4.8 — a separate, stronger model.

Getting Greedy: Generate 5 Captions and Rank Them

Ask for one caption and the AI plays it safe — it reports the biggest number. Ask for five, ranked by impact, and keep the top one:

 groundedimpact (1–5)
Ask for one92%2.56
Ask for five, keep the best73%3.86

Data only, 200‑chart pilot — same input both rows; the only change is one caption vs five ranked.

When asked for five captions, up to one or two of them are made up.

But the remaining rate higher on impact.

The fix: don't ask the LLM for the caption — ask for five ranked by impact and keep the top one. Across 200 charts, holding the input constant (data only), Impact jumps from 2.56 to 3.86 on a 5-point scale, for a 19-point drop in grounding (92 to 73). The kind of insight shifts too: single-shot picks the maximum value; ranked captions counter the default narrative and notice distributional oddities. Two judges — an impact judge and a separate Big/Useful/Surprising insight judge — independently agree on the ordering. One honest caveat: same-model judging (Sonnet generates and judges); an Opus rejudge would harden these numbers. Selection, not generation. Treat the LLM as a candidate generator, not an oracle.

Earlier Example

The same warehouse chart, fed through the pipeline. Bolder readings surface — but one of the five is quietly wrong.

Ask for one “Barbados takes more than 4× the days of Belize — 442 vs 109.” Grounded — but it only reports the biggest gap.
Ask for five · ranked #1 “Barbados (442) needs more days than the other four combined (587) — nearly double Belgium, a high‑income EU state.” ✗ Made up: 442 < 587. The model’s own numbers refute it — fluent, confident, wrong.
Ask for five · ranked #2 “Belgium, a heavily‑regulated EU economy, still takes less than half Barbados’s time (212 vs 442) — upending the idea that European red tape is the worst.” ✓ Bolder and true — the counter‑narrative.

Asking for impact surfaces sharper readings — and the occasional confident fabrication. Telling them apart is curation.

Our three charts, revisited

The room’s caption — the AI asked for one — the AI asked for five, ranked — and the reading that mattered.

Gender in the Kitchen

The room “see, men cook too.”
Ask for one “Women spend ~12.5× more time than men on meal prep — 10.2 vs 0.8 hours a week.” Grounded — the headline number.
Ask for five, ranked “Men log ~1 hour of kitchen work a week to women’s ~15 — women do roughly 94% of all kitchen labour.” Nails it — the fuller story.
The right reading women do ~12× the cooking — and almost all the serving and cleaning around it.

Questions on Stack Overflow

LinkedIn “ChatGPT killed Stack Overflow.”
Ask for one “After ChatGPT, posts fell ~90% — 3.06M in 2022 to 315k in 2025.” Grounded — but the exact LinkedIn story we opened by debunking.
Ask for five, ranked “Already down 43% before ChatGPT — 5.34M posts in 2013 to 3.06M in 2022; it accelerated an existing decline, didn’t start it.” Right — the counter‑narrative.
The right reading both series peaked years before ChatGPT. It was beating an already‑dead horse.

Health Insurance & Addiction

The room / op‑ed insurance drives intoxicant spend — moral hazard.
Ask for one “Foreign Liquor/Wine is the top intoxicant in both panels — Rs 83 per adult among the urban uninsured, the largest value shown.” Grounded — just the biggest number.
Ask for five, ranked “It’s the uninsured, not the insured, who spend more on the two priciest items — foreign liquor and beer — in both panels.” A real directional pattern, arguably sharper — yet still not the honest ‘barely matters.’
The right reading the gaps are small — insurance barely moves the needle, a non‑result that “rank by impact” will never surface.

Sooner or later, you will use LLMs to evaluate DataViz

Do

  • Ask for five, ranked by impact, and keep the best — not one shot. The prompt is the insight knob (+1.3).
  • Ground it in the numbers for fidelity, and judge with a separate, stronger model.
  • Treat the LLM as a candidate generator to curate — selection, not generation.

Don’t

  • Don’t mistake fluency for fidelity — a confident caption claimed 442 > 587.
  • Don’t trust the top-ranked caption on sight — check the arithmetic.
  • Don’t expect ranking by impact to surface a genuine non‑result.
